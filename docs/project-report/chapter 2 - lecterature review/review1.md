# Procedural Floor Plan Generation: A Review of Algorithmic Approaches and Constraints

## 1. Introduction

The automated generation of architectural spatial layouts constitutes a complex optimization challenge located at the intersection of computational geometry, graph theory, and operations research. The fundamental objective is the allocation of functional spatial units within a defined boundary while simultaneously satisfying a matrix of topological, geometric, and functional constraints. Historically, literature in this domain has heavily prioritized the rapid generation of visually plausible layouts for dynamic virtual environments and digital simulations [1], [6], [8]. However, translating these methodologies to real-world architectural applications introduces significant friction, primarily due to the stringent requirements of regulatory compliance, structural viability, and user accessibility. This review synthesizes existing algorithmic strategies, evaluating their efficacy across spatial topology, geometric fidelity, system constraints, and operational efficiency, thereby contextualizing the transition from heuristic approximations to deterministic, constraint-driven architectures.

## 2. Algorithmic Evolution and Generation Methodologies

The foundational approaches to spatial layout generation relied on exhaustive search algorithms and strict logical formalisms. Early paradigms utilized recursive depth-first tree searches on congruent-cell modular grids to systematically explore the complete solution space of rectangular mosaics [5]. While theoretically capable of ensuring strict dimensional compliance, these methodologies suffered from exponential combinatorial explosion, rendering them computationally prohibitive for configurations exceeding ten rooms or those incorporating complex, open-ended constraints [5].

To circumvent the scalability limitations of exhaustive search, contemporary research pivoted heavily toward stochastic optimization and metaheuristic algorithms. Implementations utilizing Simulated Annealing operate by mapping spatial allocations to a computational grid and applying iterative mutational operations—such as cellular addition or subtraction—evaluated against a multi-objective fitness function [3]. Similarly, other frameworks deploy the Metropolis algorithm within a simulated annealing context to iteratively refine a continuous spatial state [2], or rely on evolutionary computational models, such as the Non-dominated Sorting Genetic Algorithm II (NSGA-II), coupled with physics-inspired scalar fields [14]. Other methodologies introduce quasi-evolutionary backtracking mechanisms to traverse complex room programs [11]. While these stochastic methods demonstrate high efficacy in producing diverse, non-standardized layouts, they are fundamentally constrained by high computational overhead; generation times frequently scale from 35 seconds for highly abstracted graphical models [2] to well over an hour for detailed grid-resolution outputs [3], [14]. More critically, their reliance on probabilistic convergence provides no mathematical guarantee that strict systemic parameters will be satisfied universally.

In contrast, deterministic approaches using constraint programming models present a paradigm shift. By abstracting the generation process into an initial topological search and subsequently resolving the exact geometry via advanced mathematical solvers, systems can guarantee the satisfaction of non-negotiable parameters without the exhaustive computational degradation observed in legacy systems.

## 3. Topological Relationships and Geometric Fidelity

The accurate representation of spatial adjacencies and circulation networks is paramount to architectural viability. A prevalent technique for resolving connectivity involves the derivation of dual graphs and Properly Triangulated Planar (PTP) structures to mathematically validate spatial adjacencies prior to geometric realization [16]. Other theoretical models employ deterministic spiral-placement algorithms to achieve theoretically optimal connectivity matrices, guaranteeing exactly $3n-7$ edges for $n$ rectangular spaces [12].

However, translating these topological graphs into physical geometries frequently exposes the limitations of chosen spatial partitioning algorithms. The deployment of Squarified Treemaps, while highly efficient for hierarchical space-packing and functional zoning [1], inherently restricts the output to axis-aligned rectangular polygons [1], [9]. Slicing tree methodologies similarly confine the architectural topology to strict, sliceable geometries, often requiring substantial manual parameterization and interaction to resolve internal voids or non-orthogonal boundaries [18].

To transcend the geometric rigidity of pure rectangular packing, researchers have explored grid-based heuristic growth algorithms capable of manifesting L-shaped or non-convex rectilinear spaces [6], [8]. Nevertheless, these organic growth methodologies frequently yield impractical spatial artifacts, such as disproportionate aspect ratios or disjointed circulation corridors implicitly formed from unallocated grid cells [8], [9]. A superior architectural representation necessitates a decoupled, two-stage generation architecture where an initial graph-theoretic skeleton dictates topological relationships, followed by a secondary post-processing protocol that systematically extends boundaries to form realistic rectilinear room shapes, thereby unifying mathematical optimization with aesthetic geometric fidelity.

## 4. System Constraints and Regulatory Compliance

A critical divergence within the literature centers on the conceptualization and enforcement of systemic constraints. The majority of existing procedural generation frameworks rely on "soft" constraints or heuristic penalty functions designed to optimize for visual plausibility rather than legal necessity [2], [3]. For instance, systems calibrated for virtual environments frequently determine room inclusion and dimensional proportions based on statistical probability distributions derived from census data [1], operating entirely outside the parameters of municipal building codes.

When algorithms attempt to integrate real-world variables, they often prioritize internal functional metrics—such as optimizing nurse travel distances in healthcare facilities via spatial tree data structures and Dijkstra’s shortest path algorithm [4]—over external legal boundaries. Consequently, stochastic models occasionally terminate with unresolved spatial anomalies, including inaccessible zones or building envelope violations, requiring secondary manual intervention by domain experts [2].

For a procedural generation system to possess utility in legitimate civil planning, it must transition from probabilistic approximations to "hard" mathematical constraints. The static configuration of building regulations—encompassing precise setback distances, minimum allowable room square footages, and mandatory fenestration ratios—demands a deterministic solver architecture. Systems engineered with robust constraint programming are uniquely capable of dynamically enforcing these strict legal parameters, ensuring that the generated spatial artifacts are legally viable for subsequent engineering phases.

## 5. Operational Efficiency and User Accessibility

The final dimension of algorithmic evaluation involves operational throughput and the democratization of the technological interface. There exists a pronounced inverse correlation between computational rigor and operational velocity within the literature. While simplified heuristic grids and graph transformations can extrapolate basic layouts in milliseconds to seconds [6], [8], [11], [16], high-fidelity stochastic frameworks require extensive computational intervals—ranging from twenty to ninety minutes—to resolve complex multi-objective parameters [3], [14].

Furthermore, the operational accessibility of these systems is heavily skewed toward technical experts. High-capability procedural generators are predominantly constructed as modular plugins within professional parametric design ecosystems, such as visual programming graph interpreters, necessitating substantial architectural and computational expertise to manipulate input variables and interpret output vectors [4], [11], [14]. Conversely, systems attempting to incorporate non-expert users frequently devolve into semi-manual, highly interactive dissection tools devoid of automated optimization [18].

The synthesis of these historical limitations points toward an optimal architectural solution: an automated pipeline utilizing heuristic sampling layered upon deterministic constraint satisfaction engines. Such a paradigm is capable of generating holistic, mathematically rigorous, and regulatory-compliant rectilinear layouts within a highly constrained temporal window. By exposing this automated complexity through an abstracted presentation layer, the technology transitions from an expert-exclusive drafting utility to an accessible, pragmatic instrument for stakeholders lacking formal architectural training.

## 6. Conclusion

The current body of literature highlights a sustained tension between computational expediency, geometric expressiveness, and constraint satisfaction. While legacy algorithms and modern stochastic methodologies have successfully automated the creation of plausible spatial topologies, they fundamentally lack the mathematical rigidity required for real-world municipal compliance. Future trajectories in architectural procedural generation must prioritize decoupled, multi-stage architectures that leverage deterministic solvers to enforce strict legal constraints, thereby bridging the divide between theoretical computational geometry and practical urban development.
