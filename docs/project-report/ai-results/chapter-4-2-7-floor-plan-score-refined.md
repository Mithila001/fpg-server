### Chapter 4.2.7 Floor Plan Score

The scoring stage constitutes the principal evaluation mechanism applied after post-processing and before a generated floor plan is accepted for downstream use. Its purpose is not merely to assign a numerical value, but to determine whether the synthesized layout satisfies the minimum structural and spatial conditions required for presentation. In this sense, scoring operates as a selective filter: some layouts are rejected immediately because they fail fundamental geometric rules, while others continue into finer-grained evaluation where relative quality can be differentiated.

At a conceptual level, the scoring architecture is divided into two broad domains. The first is the critical domain, which verifies whether the floor plan reaches the minimum standard of geometric legitimacy. The second is the functional domain, which is intended to distinguish stronger layouts from weaker ones after the layout has already cleared the essential validity checks. In the current implementation stage, the functional domain remains incomplete, so the present discussion focuses primarily on the critical domain and its internal criteria.

The central scoring orchestrator serves as the decision-making core of this pipeline. It receives the normalized post-processed rooms, spatial boundary information, and requirement parameters, then evaluates them in a staged sequence. Before any meaningful score can be computed, the layout must be confirmed as internally consistent and geometrically suitable for assessment. If the normalization step produces no viable rooms, or if the resulting geometry is not sufficiently rectilinear, the process terminates immediately with a null result. This prevents invalid input from being rewarded by later stages. Once the layout passes these preliminary checks, the critical checks are applied. Their outcomes are aggregated into a fixed section score of 25 points, using a proportional relationship between the number of passed checks and the number of executed checks:

$$
S_{critical} = 25 \cdot \frac{p}{n}
$$

where $p$ denotes the number of checks that pass and $n$ denotes the total number of executed checks. This formulation has an important methodological advantage: each critical criterion contributes equally to the final result, making the evaluation transparent, reproducible, and easy to interpret. A plan that fails any critical requirement cannot be regarded as structurally reliable, regardless of how well it performs in other respects.

A gate rule then enforces the broader logic of the scoring system. If the critical section does not achieve a full score, the remaining sections are suppressed. This ensures that the system does not continue rewarding a layout whose basic structural integrity is already compromised. A second gate, applied later in the scoring sequence, requires a sufficiently strong combined performance before the more interpretive sections are allowed to contribute. In practical terms, the architecture privileges validity before refinement, which is appropriate for a generative planning system that must avoid overvaluing superficially attractive but fundamentally flawed configurations.

#### Score adjacency relations

Adjacency evaluation verifies whether the spatial relationships between rooms conform to the required relational constraints. In architectural terms, a floor plan is not only a collection of enclosed regions; it is also a network of functional proximities. Certain room types must be directly connected, while others must remain separated or only indirectly associated. The adjacency criterion encodes this logic as a set of hard requirements that are checked against the solved layout.

The evaluation process distinguishes between two kinds of relational obligations. In a conjunctive relation, every required related type must be represented by at least one contacting room. In a disjunctive relation, at least one member of the related group must establish the required contact. Contact itself is not treated loosely: it must satisfy a minimum overlap threshold so that accidental corner meeting or negligible contact does not count as a meaningful adjacency. This is an important refinement, because in spatial planning a room that merely touches another at a single point cannot reasonably be interpreted as serving the same functional connection as a room sharing a wall segment.

From an analytical perspective, adjacency scoring protects the functional coherence of the layout. It prevents configurations in which circulation would be awkward, access would be implausible, or spatial dependencies would be violated. It also supports the semantic relationship between room categories, ensuring that the layout reflects the intended logic of domestic or programmatic organization. Any failure in this category is treated as critical because adjacency is not a decorative preference; it is a structural condition that shapes usability and spatial order.

#### Score empty space

The empty-space criterion addresses internal voids that arise when the union of rectangular room regions does not form a sufficiently continuous enclosure. In a well-formed plan, the arrangement of rooms should cover the usable floor area in a coherent way. If the assembled geometry contains enclosed air gaps, those gaps represent inefficiencies, fragmentation, or unintended discontinuities in the spatial composition.

The evaluation first constructs the combined coverage of all rooms, then compares that coverage against the enclosing boundary implied by the overall arrangement. The difference between these two geometric forms reveals air gaps, which are measured by area. If the gap area exceeds the numerical tolerance threshold, the floor plan is considered to contain a meaningful enclosed void. The presence of such a void is interpreted as a failure because it indicates that the plan is not occupying space in a structurally consistent manner.

This criterion is especially significant in a generated floor plan, because algorithmic layouts can easily produce small but problematic cavities between rooms when local spatial decisions are made independently. Empty-space scoring therefore acts as a corrective mechanism, encouraging the formation of continuous and efficient coverage rather than disjointed clusters. It also reduces the likelihood of unusable residual pockets that may complicate circulation, furniture arrangement, or structural interpretation. The corresponding diagnostic output records the area of the void, the total covered area, and whether the geometry is valid, which makes the failure mode legible to both developers and reviewers.

#### Score inward pockets

The inward-pocket criterion is the most geometrically nuanced of the critical checks. Unlike adjacency or empty-space validation, which can be understood through direct relational or area-based reasoning, inward-pocket evaluation examines the shape of the plan boundary itself. Its goal is to detect excessive recesses, narrow indentations, and deeply concave protrusions that weaken the quality of the layout form.

The method begins by comparing the actual room union with its convex hull. The convex hull may be understood as the smallest convex boundary that encloses the entire layout. Any difference between this idealized envelope and the actual room union represents a pocket, that is, a concavity in the plan. Not every pocket is necessarily problematic; however, when a pocket produces a long inward segment, it can indicate an undesirable form that is difficult to use efficiently and may lead to awkward circulation or wasted perimeter.

To refine the diagnosis, each pocket is decomposed into its boundary segments and the relevant segment orientations are analyzed. The algorithm determines the dominant orientation of the hull-contact portion of the pocket and then identifies the opposing orientation among the remaining boundary segments. This step is important because it distinguishes the segments that truly project inward from those that simply trace the outer envelope. In other words, the method does not treat every concavity as equivalent. It isolates the internal wall-like segments that extend into the body of the plan and evaluates their extent more precisely.

For each identified pocket, the longest inward segment is measured and compared against a configurable maximum length. If the segment exceeds the permitted threshold, the pocket is marked as violating. The resulting diagnostics include the number of pockets, the maximum inward segment length, the orientation of the violating segment, and the coordinates of the segment endpoints. This diagnostic richness is valuable because it makes the evaluation explainable: one can see not only that the plan failed, but also why it failed and where the problematic geometry is located.

This criterion is conceptually more complex than the others because it encodes a higher-order notion of spatial quality. It is not limited to area or adjacency, but instead examines how the plan’s boundary folds inward against its own envelope. In architectural terms, this helps exclude forms with excessive articulation, sharp recesses, or thin internal notches that would reduce practical efficiency. Because of that, inward-pocket scoring plays a particularly important role in distinguishing robust plans from merely passable ones.

The functional scoring phase, which is intended to assess higher-level utility and refinement, remains under development and is therefore not examined in detail here. Nevertheless, the overall architecture already reveals a strong design principle: geometric validity is evaluated before optimization, and only plans that preserve structural coherence are allowed to advance. This staged logic is appropriate for a generative floor-plan system, because it ensures that quality assessment is anchored in spatial correctness rather than superficial visual appeal.
