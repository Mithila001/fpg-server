# Chapter 4.2.6: Post-Processing Refinement Stage

## 4.2.6.1 Overview

The post-processing stage is a critical intermediary layer between raw floor plan generation and final quality assessment. Its principal purpose is to transform geometrically plausible but still imperfect layouts into plans that are operationally usable, visually coherent, and more stable for subsequent evaluation. In practice, the generation stage can produce layouts that satisfy the main structural requirements of the model while still containing local irregularities, such as fragmented circulation zones, slightly misaligned boundaries, narrow geometric artifacts, or exterior interfaces that do not align cleanly with adjacent spaces. Rather than forcing the generation model to solve every local geometric detail directly, the refinement stage applies a controlled sequence of corrective transformations that improve the plan without fundamentally redefining its spatial logic.

This design choice is important because a floor plan generator that relies exclusively on increasingly strict constraints may become overly rigid. In that situation, the system can lose solution diversity, encounter avoidable infeasibility, or produce layouts that satisfy abstract rules but remain weak in practical usability. The refinement stage therefore acts as a balancing mechanism. It preserves the global intent of the generated plan, while correcting localized defects that would otherwise reduce the quality of the final output. The resulting system is not merely more tolerant; it is also more expressive, because the refined geometry can include non-rectangular and non-convex room forms that better reflect realistic architectural variation.

From a methodological standpoint, this stage should be understood as a conservative geometric repair pipeline. It does not attempt to reconstruct the entire plan from scratch. Instead, it performs bounded transformations that are informed by spatial context, room type, boundary adjacency, and numerical precision requirements. These transformations are especially valuable because the final scoring process can still reject poor candidates, allowing the refinement stage to improve the usable solution set without lowering the overall standard of the system.

## 4.2.6.2 Extended Wall Refinement

Extended wall refinement is one of the most influential mechanisms in the post-processing pipeline. Its purpose is to allow selected room boundaries to grow beyond their initial rectangular footprint when such an expansion improves geometric continuity or better aligns the room with neighboring spaces. This is especially significant because many generated plans begin with simplified orthogonal room blocks that are acceptable at the constraint level but too rigid to represent realistic architectural composition. By selectively extending a wall segment, the system can create richer room outlines and reduce the visual and functional limitations of strictly rectangular layouts.

The core idea is to identify candidate wall segments that can be safely expanded without violating the broader organization of the floor plan. Expansion is not applied uniformly to every boundary. Instead, it is governed by a room-type hierarchy and a set of bounded configuration parameters. The hierarchy assigns priority to certain spatial categories, allowing the system to prefer more valuable or more structurally influential rooms when deciding where refinement should occur. Configuration values further restrict the process by limiting the number of rooms eligible for expansion, the number of candidate wall selections, the proportion of a wall that may be affected, and the maximum permitted extrusion distance.

This bounded approach is essential. If wall extension were unconstrained, it could distort the plan or create large-scale geometry changes that would undermine the original solution. Instead, the system evaluates only local opportunities for improvement. When a candidate is selected, the boundary is extended outward as a geometric patch and then merged with the existing room shape. The outcome is a refined polygon that remains compatible with the original plan while gaining additional spatial depth or better adjacency relations.

The architectural benefit of this procedure is twofold. First, it improves room realism by enabling forms that are not limited to pure rectangles. Second, it supports correction of minor structural undergrowth, such as walls that stop short of an intended boundary or edges that fail to align with nearby geometry. In this sense, extended wall refinement is not merely an aesthetic enhancement; it is a practical method for resolving small layout deficiencies that would otherwise accumulate into larger quality problems.

A further advantage is that wall extension can mediate between rigid generation constraints and practical design expectations. Many architectural spaces are not naturally described by exact rectangular envelopes. Human-designed plans often contain modest protrusions, cut-ins, and aligned offsets that help accommodate circulation or adjacency needs. By introducing controlled expansion at the refinement stage, the system can approximate this richer architectural vocabulary without abandoning the discipline of computational generation.

## 4.2.6.3 Hallway Union

Hallway union addresses a common weakness in automated floor plan generation: circulation space can become fragmented into multiple adjacent segments that are logically part of the same passage but geometrically represented as separate rooms. This fragmentation is problematic because it reduces clarity, complicates scoring, and may produce an artificial impression of complexity where a single coherent hallway would be more appropriate. The union process resolves this issue by merging hallway regions that share a sufficiently long overlapping boundary.

The process relies on a geometric compatibility test. Hallway polygons are first interpreted as closed spatial regions, and their boundary intersections are examined. When the shared boundary length exceeds a minimum overlap threshold, the regions are considered to belong to the same circulation component. They are then merged into a single hallway entity. This approach is conceptually similar to clustering: individual hallway segments are grouped into a larger connected unit when their physical relationship indicates that they are functionally continuous.

The benefit of hallway union is substantial. A unified circulation zone is easier to interpret, more faithful to architectural convention, and less likely to be penalized by the later scoring stage. It also reduces redundant internal partitions, which can otherwise inflate the apparent complexity of the plan. In addition, hallway union improves downstream geometric operations because a continuous corridor is more stable than several narrow, nearly touching pieces. This stability helps later evaluation stages reason more accurately about adjacency, wall continuity, and the overall organization of the plan.

Importantly, hallway union is conservative. It does not merge spaces unless the overlap evidence is strong enough to justify the operation. That restraint prevents accidental fusion of distinct corridors or nearby rooms. As a result, the transformation improves coherence without sacrificing spatial correctness. The final output is therefore both simpler and more semantically meaningful.

From a broader design perspective, hallway union also strengthens the conceptual hierarchy of the plan. Hallways are not merely empty connective zones; they are structural mediators that organize movement through the dwelling. If a circulation route is split into multiple redundant segments, the architectural reading of the plan becomes fragmented. By merging those segments, the post-processing stage recovers the intended continuity of movement and reduces unnecessary geometric noise.

## 4.2.6.4 Snap Floor Plan to Grid

Grid snapping is a numerical refinement procedure that improves geometric consistency by aligning room coordinates to a uniform lattice. In generated floor plans, even when the overall shape is correct, the coordinate values may contain long decimal fractions caused by previous geometric operations such as union, extrusion, or intersection. These values are not necessarily wrong, but they introduce unnecessary numerical noise and can make subsequent processing less stable. Snapping resolves this by converting each boundary coordinate to the nearest grid-aligned value.

Mathematically, the transformation can be expressed as $x' = g \cdot \mathrm{round}(x / g)$ and $y' = g \cdot \mathrm{round}(y / g)$, where $g$ is the chosen grid spacing. This operation compresses floating-point variation into a regular spatial structure. The result is a plan that is easier to read, easier to compare, and less vulnerable to tiny numerical discrepancies that may otherwise accumulate across processing stages.

The practical benefit is not only visual. Grid snapping also supports consistency in geometric reasoning. When multiple rooms are expected to share boundaries or align along common axes, very small decimal offsets can prevent exact equality tests from succeeding. By normalizing the coordinates, the system reduces the likelihood of such mismatches. In addition, the plan becomes cleaner for downstream serialization, visualization, and scoring.

This process is intentionally designed so that the adjustment remains negligible at the human scale. The correction eliminates long decimal tails and minor precision drift, but it does not alter room dimensions in a meaningful architectural sense. The transformation is therefore best understood as a numerical regularization step rather than a redesign step. It preserves the intended geometry while improving the reliability of the entire processing chain.

Grid snapping also improves interoperability across separate geometric procedures. Once coordinates are regularized, the system can compare vertices, evaluate overlaps, and compute aggregate wall relations with greater confidence. This matters because post-processing often includes multiple transformation stages in sequence. If the output of one stage contains small numerical inconsistencies, those inconsistencies can amplify in later stages. Grid alignment helps prevent that accumulation and thereby increases the robustness of the whole pipeline.

## 4.2.6.5 Veranda Adjustment

The veranda adjustment stage focuses on the consistency of the exterior frontage, particularly where a veranda interacts with an outdoor transition space. Exterior appendages are sensitive to local alignment because even a small mismatch can make the frontage appear disjointed or geometrically ambiguous. The refinement process therefore checks whether the veranda and its adjoining outdoor region satisfy a direct adjacency relationship before attempting any modification.

When the two spaces are properly aligned, the exterior boundary of the veranda can be repositioned so that it conforms more naturally to the width of the adjacent outdoor zone. This creates a more coherent relationship between the built space and the open space beyond it. In architectural terms, the adjustment improves frontage continuity, reduces awkward projections, and helps the veranda read as an intentional transitional element rather than as a residual geometric artifact.

The logic behind this refinement is straightforward but important. A veranda should not be treated as an isolated rectangle detached from the rest of the plan. It is part of a larger spatial narrative connecting the interior and the exterior. By using the surrounding geometry to guide boundary placement, the post-processing stage preserves that narrative and prevents the front edge of the plan from appearing fragmented.

If the required adjacency is not present, the system avoids making an arbitrary correction. This restraint is essential because exterior refinements must be grounded in actual spatial relationships rather than in decorative assumptions. In effect, the veranda adjustment improves quality only when the geometric evidence supports it. That discipline makes the procedure robust and prevents accidental distortion of unrelated areas.

The benefit of this stage extends beyond a single room category. Exterior alignment contributes to the perceived credibility of the entire floor plan because frontage conditions are among the first features observed in an architectural layout. When the veranda and outdoor transition space are aligned well, the plan acquires a stronger sense of designed intent. This makes the final result more persuasive as both a spatial artifact and a presentation object.

## 4.2.6.6 Wall Union

Wall union consolidates overlapping or redundant boundary segments into a unified wall representation. This step is especially useful after several earlier transformations have already modified the floor plan geometry. As room shapes become more complex, multiple polygons may share edges, partially overlap, or generate duplicate boundary traces. Without consolidation, these overlaps can produce redundant wall data, which complicates later analysis and may distort measurements such as total wall length.

The union process extracts the boundary structure of the refined rooms and merges all overlapping linework into a single geometric network. From this network, the system retains only unique wall segments. The final output is therefore not simply a collection of room outlines; it is a consistent wall structure that more accurately reflects the true architectural perimeter and internal separations of the plan.

This is beneficial in several ways. First, it produces a clearer structural description of the layout, which is valuable for scoring and later interpretation. Second, it avoids double-counting walls that are shared by adjacent rooms. Third, it provides a foundation for downstream operations that depend on reliable wall information, including opening placement, circulation analysis, and boundary validation.

Wall union also contributes to the overall conceptual integrity of the post-processing stage. The floor plan is no longer represented as a loose collection of room polygons; it becomes a coherent spatial system with a unified boundary logic. That shift is important because it bridges the gap between local room refinement and global architectural readability.

A further advantage is that wall union creates a more stable structural abstraction for later decision-making. Once redundant segments are removed, the remaining wall network is easier to interpret as a set of functional edges rather than as a collection of accidental geometric overlaps. This improves both quantitative evaluation and qualitative presentation. In effect, wall union turns a potentially noisy geometric result into a disciplined architectural surface.

## 4.2.6.7 Overall Evaluation of the Refinement Pipeline

Taken together, the post-processing procedures form a layered correction strategy. Extended wall refinement improves local room geometry. Hallway union reduces fragmentation in circulation space. Grid snapping regularizes coordinates and suppresses precision drift. Veranda adjustment strengthens exterior coherence. Wall union consolidates the resulting geometry into a reliable structural representation. Each step addresses a distinct class of defect, yet all of them serve the same larger objective: to transform a technically feasible floor plan into a higher-quality architectural candidate.

The value of this pipeline lies in its balance between flexibility and control. The refinement stage is powerful enough to correct small defects and enrich the resulting geometry, but it is still constrained enough to preserve the original generation logic. This is a crucial distinction. The goal is not to replace constraint-based generation with unrestricted geometric editing. The goal is to supplement the generator with a disciplined repair mechanism that improves usability without sacrificing structural validity.

In practice, this approach offers a strong compromise. It reduces the need to make the generation model excessively restrictive, thereby avoiding unnecessary infeasibility, while still ensuring that the final plans meet a higher standard of spatial quality. The scoring stage remains responsible for filtering weak candidates, so the refinement pipeline can focus on constructive improvement rather than hard rejection. The combined effect is a more resilient floor plan generation framework, capable of producing results that are both mathematically consistent and architecturally meaningful.

The importance of this layered design should not be underestimated. Many automated layout systems fail because they attempt to encode every desirable property directly into the generative constraints. That strategy can be effective for simple cases, but it often collapses when the layout must satisfy many interacting geometric expectations at once. By distributing responsibility across generation, refinement, and scoring, the system reduces pressure on any single stage and thereby improves overall reliability.

## 4.2.6.8 Chapter Ending

The post-processing stage is therefore a decisive component of the floor plan generation system. It demonstrates that high-quality architectural output does not depend solely on strict generation constraints, but also on careful geometric refinement after generation has occurred. By integrating selective wall extension, hallway consolidation, coordinate snapping, veranda adjustment, and wall union, the system converts imperfect raw layouts into refined floor plans with greater coherence and practical value.

This chapter has shown that refinement is not an auxiliary convenience; it is an essential methodological layer. It improves the interpretability of the plan, strengthens numerical stability, and increases the likelihood that the resulting geometry will be accepted by later scoring mechanisms. Most importantly, it allows the system to preserve creative spatial variation while still maintaining a disciplined and measurable architectural structure.

In summary, the post-processing framework acts as a bridge between algorithmic feasibility and architectural quality. It is the stage at which the floor plan becomes more than a mathematically admissible arrangement of spaces and begins to function as a coherent design artifact. That transformation is central to the success of the overall system.
