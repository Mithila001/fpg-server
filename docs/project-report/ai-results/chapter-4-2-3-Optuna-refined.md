### Chapter 4.2.3 Probabilistic Hint Sampling via Adaptive Search (Optuna)

The first optimization stage in the floor plan generation pipeline is designed to produce spatial hints rather than final room geometry. Its purpose is to search for a favorable arrangement of candidate locations that can later be interpreted by the constraint-based solver as soft guidance. In this role, the optimization engine functions as a probabilistic search mechanism over a high-dimensional layout space, where each trial proposes a possible configuration of point-based hints inside the floor boundary. The solver is not bypassed; instead, it is reserved for the more promising candidates produced by the exploration phase.

This architectural decision emerged from a practical constraint. Directly optimizing full room dimensions through the solver required substantial time per trial, because each complete attempt combined generation, post-processing, and scoring into a relatively expensive cycle. Under that regime, only a limited number of trials could be executed within the available runtime budget. That was insufficient for the optimization process to accumulate the diversity of observations needed for stable learning. The revised strategy therefore shifts the optimization target away from exact room construction and toward the generation of hint points. By reducing the computational burden of each trial, the system can explore a much larger number of candidates within the same time envelope.

The resulting workflow may be understood as a two-level search. At the first level, the sampler proposes a set of coordinates for the relevant spatial entities. These coordinates are not fixed placements; rather, they represent preferred regions from which the downstream solver may infer room arrangement. At the second level, the solver evaluates whether the hinted configuration can be realized under the geometric and relational constraints of the project. This separation between exploratory sampling and exact generation makes the process significantly more efficient, because the expensive solving step is applied only after a candidate has already demonstrated structural promise.

A key characteristic of the search space is its reduced resolution. Instead of searching continuously over every possible coordinate, the sampler operates on a discretized lattice defined by a fixed step size. If the floor extent is denoted by $W$ and $H$, and the grid spacing by $\Delta$, then candidate positions are constrained to values of the form $x = x_0 + k\Delta$ and $y = y_0 + m\Delta$, where $k$ and $m$ are integers. This scaling-down strategy serves several purposes. First, it lowers the number of admissible states, which increases the likelihood that a limited number of trials will revisit structurally meaningful regions of the search space. Second, it ensures that sampled hints remain separated by a practical distance, preventing the optimization process from wasting effort on nearly identical coordinates. Third, it aligns the sampler with the granularity expected by the solver, thereby reducing the mismatch between exploratory hints and executable layouts.

The sampling parameters are therefore best understood as spatial bounds, grid resolution, and hint radius. The spatial bounds define the admissible region within the floor envelope so that generated points remain geometrically valid. The grid resolution determines how finely the space is partitioned, and the hint radius establishes the local neighborhood around each proposed point. Together, these parameters shape the search landscape so that the optimization engine focuses on broad structural relations rather than excessive geometric precision. In an architectural context, this is appropriate because the early-stage goal is not to exact a final blueprint, but to identify a coherent spatial skeleton.

Each trial returns a quantitative evaluation of the sampled hint arrangement. In the first stage, this evaluation is based on the internal scoring logic that measures how well the proposed hint map supports a plausible layout. The score is continuous rather than binary, which is important because it allows the sampler to distinguish between weak, moderate, and strong candidates. A binary outcome would collapse meaningful nuance and make the learning process less informative. By contrast, a graded score creates a smoother optimization surface and enables the sampler to adapt toward more promising regions over successive trials.

When a trial exceeds the solver gate, the system proceeds to a second evaluation phase. At that point, the sampled hints are passed into the solver as soft guidance. If the solver successfully constructs a valid floor plan, the result from the solver is combined with the earlier sampling score to produce a composite assessment. If the solver does not succeed, the trial still contributes to the search history through the score assigned at the sampling stage. This design preserves information even from partial failures, which is useful because a failed solver attempt may still reveal that the underlying hint geometry was structurally promising or close to feasible.

The sampler itself is used in a focused manner. Its role is not to exhaustively model every architectural rule, but to learn from previous candidate quality and bias subsequent trials toward more useful point patterns. The benefit of this approach lies in its ability to concentrate exploration on high-value regions of the search space while maintaining enough stochastic variation to avoid premature convergence. In practical terms, the sampler tends to improve the quality of hint layouts over time because it is guided by the observed relationship between sampled coordinates and downstream layout success.

Another advantage of the coarse lattice is that it implicitly introduces a form of spatial regularization. Extremely dense sampling often encourages redundant trials that differ only by negligible offsets, which contributes little to learning while consuming time budget. By contrast, a discretized coordinate system forces the search to make more substantive moves between trials. This makes the optimization trajectory easier to interpret and more efficient in practice, because each new sample is more likely to test a genuinely distinct structural hypothesis about the floor plan. The result is not merely faster execution, but a more meaningful distribution of explored candidates across the available plan area.

From a procedural perspective, each trial can be described as a sequence of three conceptual operations. First, the sampler proposes a coordinate set inside the validated envelope. Second, the candidate is evaluated against the project’s structural heuristics to determine whether it is sufficiently promising. Third, if the candidate passes the internal gate, the solver receives the corresponding hints and attempts exact realization. This sequence ensures that the most computationally expensive stage is reserved for layouts that have already demonstrated some degree of coherence. The optimization loop therefore behaves as a progressive filter, gradually narrowing the field from broad spatial possibility to concrete geometric feasibility.

Persistent storage is disabled by default in the current workflow. This choice is consistent with the exploratory nature of the optimization stage. Since the search target is not a single fixed solution but a family of admissible spatial hint configurations, the system favors fresh sampling runs over rigid reuse of prior studies. This makes the optimization process more adaptable to changing room requirements, boundary dimensions, and layout goals.

It is also important to note that not every theoretical capability of the optimization framework is central to the present implementation. Features that are not actively used in the current workflow are intentionally excluded from the description of the core method. The present design emphasizes the practical interplay between coarse spatial sampling, gate-based solver invocation, and score-driven refinement. That combination is what enables the system to move from an expensive direct-search model toward a more scalable and experimentally efficient optimization pipeline.

In summary, the optimization stage acts as a bridge between abstract spatial exploration and exact constraint-based generation. By transforming the problem from full geometry synthesis into hint discovery, the system achieves a substantially larger trial budget, a more informative learning signal, and a better match between exploratory search and solver execution. The low-resolution search space, continuous scoring, and selective solver invocation together form a coherent methodology for producing floor plan candidates that are both computationally tractable and structurally meaningful.
