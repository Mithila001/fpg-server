### Chapter 3.2.5 Constraint-Based Floor Plan Generation Framework

The floor plan generator is the computational core of the project. It translates architectural intent into a feasible arrangement of rectangular rooms under geometric, relational, and stylistic constraints. It is also the most computationally demanding stage, because it must satisfy hard feasibility conditions while still preserving design intent and allowing diverse layouts.

At a conceptual level, the generator is built on a constraint programming model solved through a CP-SAT engine. This is appropriate because floor planning is a combinatorial placement problem governed by exact relational rules rather than continuous optimization alone. Rooms must not overlap, required adjacencies must hold, frontage rules must be preserved, and the final arrangement must remain flexible enough to support multiple architectural interpretations. The solver therefore balances strict admissibility with guided exploration.

The coordinate system is central to how the rules are interpreted. The origin lies at the bottom-left of the floor plate, the horizontal axis runs left to right, and the vertical axis represents depth from the frontage toward the interior. In this orientation, $y=0$ corresponds to the front edge of the plan. This convention matters because several constraints reason directly about entrance depth, frontage preservation, and placement near the exterior boundary. Geometric optimization is therefore semantically directional rather than symmetric.

The generation pipeline is organized into profiles. The primary generation profile produces a fresh feasible layout from a requirement set. The refinement profile accepts an existing layout and improves it through a bounded re-solve that preserves overall composition while adjusting local relationships. This staged structure is important because it prevents the solver from treating all layouts as equally free to change. Instead, each pass has a distinct objective: first feasibility, then controlled improvement.

The configuration panel acts as a constraint selection matrix. It enables or disables families of rules without changing the solver architecture. This makes the generator experimentally flexible and analytically transparent. Different profiles are therefore configurations of the same spatial reasoning engine rather than separate solvers.

#### Computational Core and Solver Logic

The core engine assembles the full constraint model, declares decision variables for each room, injects the selected rules, and instructs the solver to search for a feasible or improved arrangement. Before optimization can begin, every room must be represented as a rectangle with explicit position, size, and boundary variables. The model then interprets the plan as a coupled system of integer decisions.

Initialization begins by normalizing the requirement data and preparing the room set for solver use. This includes system-generated rooms such as the living area and required circulation elements. The floor plan is therefore not composed only of user-specified rooms, but also of anchor spaces introduced automatically so the solver can reason over the complete topology.

For each room, the engine creates decision variables for the left coordinate, bottom coordinate, width, height, right boundary, upper boundary, and area. These are linked through exact arithmetic relations so that each room remains internally consistent. Every room is thus treated as a constrained geometric object rather than a free-form polygon. Rectangular decision variables simplify the problem while still supporting rich spatial reasoning.

The engine applies constraints in layers. First, it establishes geometric feasibility. Next, it introduces domain-specific rules governing hallways, shared walls, required adjacencies, frontage anchors, and special placement behavior for key rooms. Finally, it adds objective terms when refinement-oriented or soft-guided configurations are active. This order is necessary because hard feasibility must be secured before softer preferences can be evaluated.

The solver is intentionally randomized within bounded time. A time cap prevents unbounded effort on a single layout, while randomized search parameters encourage diversity across runs. This is valuable in floor planning, where multiple plausible solutions may exist and the aim is not always a single optimal arrangement, but a strong compromise between feasibility, usability, and architectural character.

The final objective is a weighted sum of the active soft penalties:

$$
\min \sum_{k=1}^{n} \lambda_k C_k
$$

where each $C_k$ is a soft cost component and $\lambda_k$ is its corresponding weight. If no soft terms are enabled, the solver performs a pure feasibility search. Otherwise, it minimizes the combined soft cost while satisfying all hard requirements.

#### Phase I: Feasibility-Driven Synthesis

The initialization profile builds a fresh floor plan from requirement data. Its purpose is not to polish an existing arrangement, but to establish a valid structural baseline. In practice, this profile emphasizes hard feasibility and only a minimal set of soft biases, making it the most conservative profile in the system.

A notable feature of this profile is the use of point-based hints to derive an initial spatial tendency. These hints are not hard geometric commitments. They provide the solver with a plausible starting direction derived from the input requirements, helping it begin the search in a relevant region of the solution space while still allowing alternatives when needed. The hints therefore serve as initialization guidance rather than rigid design locks.

The initialization profile focuses on the hard conditions necessary for validity. The generated layout must respect non-overlap, room shape limits, adjacency logic, and frontage semantics before stylistic concerns are considered. This reflects a basic architectural principle: a plan must first be admissible before it can be refined aesthetically.

Because the generation profile is meant to establish the first workable layout, it avoids heavy dependence on seed-based penalties. If the first pass were overly constrained by prior arrangement logic, it could fail to discover a legitimate baseline. The profile therefore prioritizes feasibility, structural coherence, and broad search freedom within the legal design envelope.

#### Phase II: Iterative Layout Refinement

The refinement profile is the second major phase of the generator. Whereas initialization seeks a valid arrangement from scratch, refinement assumes a layout already exists and attempts to improve it without destroying its overall structure. This is a local-search strategy in which the solver reuses an existing solution as context and searches for a better nearby variant.

Its distinctive feature is bounded movement. Rather than allowing unrestricted repositioning, the solver is given limited spatial wiggle around the prior solution. This ensures the output remains recognizably related to the original plan. Such bounded adjustment is useful when the goal is iterative design development, where each pass should polish the composition rather than replace it wholesale.

Refinement also introduces a richer set of soft preferences. These do not block feasibility, but they alter the ranking of candidate layouts. The solver is therefore encouraged to preserve useful alignments, reduce wasted area, maintain facade clarity, and improve shared-wall quality. The profile is thus less about satisfying constraints and more about increasing the architectural maturity of an already viable plan.

The refinement process can be applied repeatedly. Each pass may receive the output of the previous pass, creating a multi-stage improvement sequence. This is useful because some spatial qualities emerge gradually: one pass may recover structure, another compactness, and another facade order. The solver architecture supports this layered improvement without requiring a separate algorithm for each pass.

The refinement profile also reflects an important system philosophy: not all constraints should be treated equally in every phase. Early on, feasibility dominates. Later, a richer set of descriptive preferences can be activated. This staged approach reduces the risk of overconstraining the solver too soon and improves the interpretability of the final output.

#### Constraint Formalization and Taxonomy

The constraint system is the main mechanism through which architectural intent is translated into an admissible spatial configuration. It is best understood as a two-tier taxonomy. Hard constraints establish feasibility and define the non-negotiable spatial envelope. Soft constraints act as preference terms that influence ranking among otherwise valid candidates. The distinction is essential: violating a hard constraint makes a plan invalid, while violating a soft constraint only reduces desirability.

The discussion below excludes auxiliary extender behavior, since that pathway belongs to a separate architectural branch and is not part of the main generation and refinement narrative.

##### Hard Constraints

Hard constraints ensure structural admissibility. They keep rooms geometrically consistent, functionally connected, and directionally ordered in a manner compatible with residential planning conventions. Together, these rules prevent mathematically possible but architecturally incoherent layouts.

###### Geometric Feasibility and Rectangular Consistency

Geometric feasibility rules establish the lowest level of validity. They prevent overlap, preserve coordinate consistency, and ensure that each room’s boundary values remain coherent with its declared position and dimensions. Aspect-ratio bounds also constrain width-to-height relations so that excessively thin or flattened shapes do not appear under ordinary conditions.

This family is foundational because every later rule depends on a reliable notion of spatial extent. If rooms can intersect or violate their boundary logic, adjacency and frontage reasoning lose meaning. The model must first establish where each room begins and ends before higher-order relationships can be evaluated.

Certain room types receive differentiated proportional tolerance because their functional geometry differs from that of conventional interior spaces. Garages and verandas may justifiably be more elongated than standard enclosed rooms, preventing layouts that are admissible in theory but implausible in practice.

###### Circulation Spine Morphology and Connectivity

Circulation constraints ensure that hall-like spaces behave as genuine passage elements rather than arbitrary rectangular fillers. A hallway must preserve a narrow dimension within a controlled range and a sufficiently extended complementary dimension so its linear character remains clear.

Connectivity is equally important. The circulation space must connect with the principal living area and at least one additional non-living room, either directly or through a controlled chain of similar spaces. This prevents circulation from becoming isolated or functionally inert. The hallway therefore acts as connective tissue within the spatial organization of the house.

The rule set also evaluates boundary-sharing behavior. A hallway should retain a minimum number of fully shared sides with neighboring rooms, encouraging integration into the composition rather than floating as an isolated void.

###### Boundary Coupling and Enclosure Requirements

Boundary-coupling rules regulate how strongly a room participates in the enclosing fabric of the plan. The model allows each room category to express its own expectations regarding the number of sides that should be fully shared with adjacent spaces, which helps distinguish public, private, and transitional zones.

The hard version of this rule counts fully shared sides and checks whether the selected sides satisfy type-specific minimum and maximum thresholds. A controlled relaxation margin may be admitted through a wiggle percentage, allowing near-complete alignments when coverage is sufficiently close. This preserves robustness without sacrificing precision.

This family also influences whether a room feels embedded within the plan or exposed at its margins. Rooms with stronger boundary-coupling requirements tend to occupy deeper positions, while rooms with looser requirements may remain nearer the perimeter.

###### Mandatory Functional Adjacency

Functional adjacency rules are derived from relation data expressing indispensable connections between room categories. The solver distinguishes between direct mandatory relations and broader alternative relations. In the former case, a room must adjoin one or more specified targets; in the latter, adjacency may be satisfied by any compatible member of a permitted set.

This is not a simple point-contact rule. It also uses a minimum overlap threshold so that accidental contact is not mistaken for meaningful adjacency. Functional relationships in residential planning generally require a substantial shared boundary rather than negligible geometric coincidence.

These rules define the functional graph of the house. They determine which rooms can plausibly communicate and which separations would be excessive.

###### Front-to-Back Spatial Hierarchy

Front-to-back spatial hierarchy introduces macro-level directional order into the plan. The frontage side is treated as the front of the house, and spaces that mediate that frontage must remain closer to the exterior edge than deeper interior rooms. When a veranda exists, it becomes the primary anchor; otherwise, the living area serves that role.

This rule prevents frontage logic from becoming inverted or ambiguous. The plan should not be seen as an undifferentiated set of adjacent rooms, but as a depth hierarchy moving from public entry toward private interior. The rule enforces this hierarchy through geometric comparison of room centers.

Its effect is subtle but important. By stabilizing front-to-back ordering, the model reduces symmetry-induced drift and strengthens the legibility of the arrangement.

###### Frontage Interface Regulation

Frontage interface regulation governs the veranda as a mediator between the house and the exterior. The veranda is fixed to the front boundary so that it clearly articulates the relationship between interior occupation and external access. The model also creates an auxiliary outdoor-space region associated with the veranda, preserving the intended frontage composition.

The veranda must attach along one side to this outdoor region, and the region itself is positioned so that it does not overlap with other actual rooms. The frontage is therefore treated as a structured interface with reserved geometry rather than an abstract line. The veranda functions as a frontage organizer rather than a mere room category.

###### Vehicular Annex Placement and Access

Vehicular annex placement regulates the garage through both anchoring and access logic. The garage must lie near exactly one lateral boundary, either left or right, which establishes a clear vehicular orientation and avoids ambiguous central placement.

Access is equally important. The garage must either sit directly on the front boundary or receive access through an aligned frontage segment. The rule therefore combines placement logic with circulation semantics.

###### Perimeter Staggering and Setback Regulation

Perimeter staggering shapes the outer massing of the plan by encouraging controlled setback behavior along selected exterior sides. Instead of every room aligning flush with the envelope, the solver is guided toward stepped edges with deliberate offsets.

The rule relies on the principle that rooms exposed to the exterior should maintain a positive clearance relationship with neighboring rooms along designated sides. The result is not a rigid perimeter polygon, but a sequence of controlled displacements that introduce variation into the outline.

###### Service-Side Clearance Preservation

Service-side clearance preservation requires at least one eligible kitchen or circulation back wall to retain a clear setback behind it. The purpose is to avoid placing a functional rear wall too close to the interior boundary, where door clearance and movement depth may become inadequate.

The rule examines the distance from the relevant back wall to the floor boundary and checks whether the adjacent zone remains free from intrusive room geometry. It creates a protected depth band that improves usability without imposing a blanket setback across the entire plan.

##### Soft Constraints

Soft constraints do not determine admissibility; they shape the solver’s preference landscape. They guide the search toward layouts that are valid, compact, and semantically legible. These terms are especially important during refinement, where the goal is to improve an already feasible arrangement without destroying its structural identity.

###### Adjacency Affinity Preference

Adjacency affinity uses the same relational logic as mandatory adjacency, but translates the result into a preference signal rather than a feasibility requirement. The solver can therefore benefit from adjacency information without being restricted to a narrow set of exact placements.

###### Plan Compactness and Centrality

The compactness objective encourages rooms to remain near the horizontal center of the floor plate while biasing the arrangement toward the front-oriented side of the coordinate frame. It combines a horizontal deviation term with a depth-related term: the horizontal component discourages excessive lateral spread, while the vertical component shapes the front-to-interior balance.

The result is a plan that tends to cluster more tightly around the central axis, improving coherence when hard constraints leave substantial freedom.

###### Private Sanitary Zone Depth Preference

The sanitary-zone preference rewards deeper placement for bathrooms and related private service spaces. Because larger $y$ values correspond to greater interior depth, the penalty decreases when such spaces are moved away from the frontage.

This does not forbid frontward placement when necessary, but it does encourage more private positioning whenever the rest of the plan allows it.

###### Residual Void Minimization

The residual void penalty measures empty area inside the bounding box enclosing all rooms. If the rooms occupy a compact envelope, the bounding box closely approximates the total room area; if they are widely dispersed, the residual void increases. The objective therefore penalizes inefficient internal emptiness.

Formally, if $A_{bbox}$ is the area of the enclosing rectangle and $\sum_i A_i$ is the total room area, the residual void may be written as

$$
D = A_{bbox} - \sum_i A_i
$$

The solver minimizes a weighted version of $D$, encouraging rooms to occupy their shared envelope efficiently rather than leaving large unused pockets.

###### Seed-Conditioned Positional Guidance

Seed-conditioned guidance transfers prior design information into the solver. When a seed arrangement is available, the solver may receive suggested positions and dimensions for matching rooms. These values are not hard commitments, but they strongly bias the search trajectory.

This is especially useful during refinement, where the intention is to preserve useful structure while allowing limited movement. A small wiggle band may be introduced around the seed values so the solver can adjust the layout without abandoning its overall geometry.

###### Facade Depth Conservation

Facade depth conservation preserves the prominence of rooms previously aligned with the outer facade. It measures how far those rooms recede from the original frontage line and penalizes excessive inward movement.

This term is useful when the plan already exhibits a clear frontage rhythm that should not be lost during refinement. It helps maintain the visible ordering of front-facing and boundary-facing rooms.

###### Boundary Alignment Conservation

Boundary alignment conservation preserves edge harmony among rooms that were aligned in the seed layout. When two rooms were already close in boundary position and the seed overlap suggests a meaningful visual relationship, the solver is penalized if those edges drift apart.

This is not exact replication, but protection of local alignment patterns that contribute to architectural legibility. The effect is especially relevant for facade composition, where small edge shifts can noticeably alter appearance.

###### Recess Modulation of the Frontage Plane

Recess modulation is a more expressive form of frontage quality control. It distinguishes between shallow and severe recesses and applies different weight levels accordingly. It also introduces an attachment-oriented component that discourages nearby frontage elements from losing relative alignment.

This produces a more refined notion of facade quality than a simple depth cap. The model asks how far, how severely, and in what local context a room is recessed, which helps preserve architectural rhythm while permitting modest variation.

###### Boundary-Coupling Reinforcement

Boundary-coupling reinforcement transforms the shared-wall expectation from a hard admissibility condition into a softer improvement target. During refinement, rooms are penalized when they fail to meet the tighter shared-boundary minimum associated with their type. Instead of rejection, the solver receives a graded cost signal.

This is a strong example of stage-sensitive design. During generation, shared boundaries help define validity; during refinement, the same spatial idea becomes a quality metric that can be improved without forcing infeasibility.

#### End of This Chapter

The floor plan generator is the most structurally demanding part of the project because it must reconcile geometry, function, and design intent within one optimization framework. Its strength lies in the coordinated interaction among hard feasibility constraints, staged refinement, and soft architectural preferences. The generation profile establishes a valid spatial foundation, the refinement profile polishes that foundation with limited local movement, and the constraint architecture ensures that the resulting layout remains both practical and expressive.

Viewed as a whole, the chapter shows that successful floor planning is not simply a matter of drawing rectangles. It is a disciplined process of encoding architectural meaning into mathematical form and allowing the solver to search for the most coherent arrangement that satisfies that meaning. The generator achieves this by combining strict geometric legality with configurable spatial intelligence, making it the conceptual center of the system.
