### Chapter 3.2.7 Floor Plan Score

The scoring stage is the principal evaluation step applied after post-processing and before a generated floor plan is accepted for downstream use. Its role is not only to assign a numerical value, but to determine whether the synthesized layout satisfies the minimum structural and spatial conditions required for presentation. It therefore acts as a selective filter: some layouts are rejected immediately because they fail basic geometric rules, while others proceed to a more interpretive functional assessment.

The scoring architecture now has two broad domains. The first is the critical domain, which checks whether the floor plan reaches the minimum standard of geometric legitimacy. The second is a basic functional domain, which distinguishes stronger layouts from weaker ones after essential validity checks have been passed. The critical layer remains decisive, but the functional layer now contributes a limited supplementary score once the plan has already satisfied the core geometric gate.

The central scoring orchestrator receives the normalized post-processed rooms, spatial boundary information, and requirement parameters, then evaluates them in sequence. Before a meaningful score can be computed, the layout must be internally consistent and geometrically suitable for assessment. If normalization produces no viable rooms, or if the resulting geometry is not sufficiently rectilinear, the process terminates immediately with an empty scoring result. This prevents invalid input from being rewarded by later stages. Once the layout passes these preliminary checks, the critical checks are applied. Their outcomes are aggregated into a fixed section score of 25 points using:

$$
S_{critical} = 25 \cdot \frac{p}{n}
$$

where $p$ is the number of passed checks and $n$ is the total number of executed checks. This formulation gives each critical criterion equal weight and keeps the evaluation transparent and reproducible. A plan that fails any critical requirement cannot be considered structurally reliable, regardless of its performance elsewhere.

A gate rule then enforces the broader logic of the scoring system. If the critical section does not achieve a full score, the remaining sections are suppressed. When the critical score is perfect, the basic functional section may contribute an additional 75 points, yielding a combined total of 100. This design privileges validity before refinement, which is appropriate for a generative planning system.

#### Score adjacency relations

Adjacency evaluation verifies whether spatial relationships between rooms satisfy the required relational constraints. Some room types must be directly connected, while others must remain separated or only indirectly associated. The criterion encodes this as a set of hard requirements.

Two relational forms are distinguished. In a conjunctive relation, every required related type must be represented by at least one contacting room. In a disjunctive relation, at least one member of the related group must establish the required contact. Contact must also satisfy a minimum overlap threshold so that corner touching or negligible contact does not count as meaningful adjacency. This protects the functional coherence of the layout and prevents awkward circulation or violated spatial dependencies.

#### Score empty space

The empty-space criterion addresses internal voids that appear when the union of rectangular room regions does not form a sufficiently continuous enclosure. The evaluation compares the combined coverage of all rooms against the enclosing boundary implied by the overall arrangement. Any difference reveals air gaps, which are measured by area. If the gap area exceeds the tolerance threshold, the floor plan is considered to contain a meaningful enclosed void and fails this criterion.

This check is important in generated layouts because local spatial decisions can create small but problematic cavities. Empty-space scoring therefore encourages continuous coverage and reduces unusable residual pockets. The diagnostic output records the void area, total covered area, and whether the geometry is valid.

#### Score inward pockets

The inward-pocket criterion is the most geometrically nuanced critical check. It examines the plan boundary itself to detect excessive recesses, narrow indentations, and deeply concave protrusions. The method compares the room union with its convex hull, and any difference represents a pocket. Each pocket is decomposed into boundary segments, and the dominant hull-contact orientation is compared with the opposing orientation among the remaining segments.

For each pocket, the longest inward segment is measured and compared against a configurable maximum length. If it exceeds the threshold, the pocket is marked as violating. The diagnostics include the number of pockets, the maximum inward segment length, the violating orientation, and the segment endpoints. This makes the evaluation explainable and indicates precisely where the problematic geometry occurs.

#### Basic functional score

The new basic functional stage provides a compact qualitative assessment after geometric validity has been established. It examines three high-level room relationships that are central to residential usability. First, the principal living area is evaluated for proportional balance against the remaining rooms, so that the communal core does not become excessively dominant. Second, bedroom areas are assessed for both minimum adequacy and internal consistency, with a stronger penalty applied when any bedroom falls below the expected area threshold or when bedroom sizes vary too widely. Third, the relationship between the kitchen and dining area is measured through direct adjacency when available, or through spatial separation as a fallback indicator when direct contact is absent.

The three sub-scores are weighted equally and normalized to a 75-point functional margin. This keeps the supplement easy to interpret while preserving the primacy of the critical gate. Overall, the architecture ensures that geometric validity is evaluated before refinement, and only structurally coherent plans are allowed to advance to the final functional review.

#### Brief summary

The scoring system therefore combines hard geometric validation with a smaller functional layer. Plans must first satisfy adjacency, empty-space, and inward-pocket constraints before the new functional measures can influence the final result. This arrangement preserves structural reliability while still allowing stronger layouts to receive additional recognition.
