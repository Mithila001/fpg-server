# Appendix: Wall Extension as Conservative Geometric Refinement

The wall-extension stage operates as a post-generation refinement layer. Its purpose is not to redesign the plan, but to make locally feasible improvements where a boundary can be safely prolonged into nearby empty space. In architectural terms, this is a controlled repair mechanism: it helps a room recover continuity, strengthens adjacency, and reduces irregular gaps without altering the overall spatial program.
The method begins from the current set of room polygons and recomputes the empty spaces around them. Two families of candidate spaces are then distinguished. The first consists of internal voids, which are enclosed gaps within the assembled floor plate. The second consists of external recesses, which are indentation-like empty regions along the outer envelope. A wall segment becomes eligible only when it intersects one of these spaces and satisfies the room-specific length bounds. This ensures that the extension logic remains selective rather than indiscriminate.
Candidate walls are ranked in a hierarchy of programmatic importance. The system processes room categories in the order shown in Table A1. This ordering is significant because earlier categories are given the first opportunity to claim suitable expansion opportunities. Within each category, smaller rooms are examined first, which reflects the assumption that compact spaces often benefit most from controlled geometric recovery. The number of rooms examined is also capped, so the process remains bounded even in dense layouts.

## Table A1. Hierarchy and configuration of the wall-extension stage

| Room category | Priority order | Minimum wall length | Maximum wall length | Maximum rooms considered | Maximum selected segments | Expansion ratio | Maximum extrusion distance |
| ------------- | -------------: | ------------------: | ------------------: | -----------------------: | ------------------------: | --------------: | -------------------------: |
| Veranda       |              1 |                  10 |                  50 |                        3 |                         1 |            0.80 |                         40 |
| Living room   |              2 |                  10 |                  40 |                        1 |                         2 |            0.80 |                         20 |
| Kitchen       |              3 |                  10 |                  40 |                        1 |                         1 |            0.80 |                         10 |
| Hallway       |              4 |                   5 |                  50 |                        3 |                         3 |            0.80 |                          2 |
| Bedroom       |              5 |                  10 |                  40 |                        3 |                         1 |            0.80 |                         10 |

The table shows a clear pattern. The veranda receives the largest possible extrusion range because it is the most permissive frontage-oriented element in the refinement hierarchy. The living room also receives substantial flexibility, although its maximum distance is more restrained. The kitchen is treated conservatively, since excessive protrusion can weaken its compact functional form. Hallways are allowed the largest number of selected segments, but the actual extrusion distance is very small; this reflects the need to improve circulation continuity without turning a corridor into an oversized chamber. Bedrooms are expanded cautiously, with multiple candidates allowed but only one segment ultimately selected. Across all room categories, the expansion ratio is $0.80$, meaning that the proposed extrusion distance is derived as approximately $0.8\ell$, where $\ell$ is the candidate wall length, and then limited by the maximum permitted distance for that room type.

The geometric refinement itself follows a careful directional test. For each eligible wall segment, the system first determines whether the segment is oriented more horizontally or more vertically. It then probes the two perpendicular directions and chooses the side that best corresponds to the adjacent empty space. In other words, the method seeks the direction in which the wall can grow into the available void rather than into occupied geometry. If the initial containment probe is inconclusive, the system uses a small intersection-based comparison to identify the stronger candidate direction.
Once the direction is chosen, the extension distance is refined through a bounded search procedure. The candidate extrusion is tested repeatedly until the largest safe shift is found within the allowed range. This produces a stable geometric result because the final wall growth is not estimated by a single abrupt jump; instead, it is approximated through gradual narrowing. The refined boundary is then assembled as a polygonal patch and intersected with the target empty region so that the final correction remains fully compatible with the surrounding geometry.
Several safeguards prevent the process from becoming over-aggressive. Very short segments are ignored entirely. Segments longer than the permitted maximum are truncated before evaluation. Internal voids are preferred over external recesses when ranking candidates, because enclosed gaps generally represent stronger opportunities for improvement. These rules give the algorithm a conservative character: it may enhance the quality of a plan, but it does not create new architectural intent.
From a thesis perspective, the main contribution of this stage is its balance between structure and restraint. It improves the legibility of the floor plan, supports cleaner adjacency, and reduces visually unstable fragments, while preserving the design’s original organization. The result is a plan that is more coherent for subsequent cleaning, snapping, and scoring operations.
